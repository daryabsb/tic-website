# Discovery questionnaire, per-office inputs, structure validation and comment-collection workflow for the TIC website rebuild

Research date: 2026-10-21. Researcher notes for the TIC Quality Control (ticqc.ae) website-rebuild proposal. Scope: how to run the internal consultation (questionnaires, office inputs, validating menus and service names with executives, collecting and approving comments across 13 offices and two languages). This file is research only. It is not the questionnaire itself and it designs nothing.

How to read this file: each key question has a Takeaway, Cited Findings (every claim carries the URL it came from), Inferences (marked as mine) and Gaps. Vendor pages are labelled "vendor". The appendices at the end give (a) the question bank table, (b) the per-office content inventory field list, (c) comparison tables for validation methods and comment-collection methods, and (d) every URL visited with its status.

Notes on evidence: the web-search budget ran out part-way through this task, so later steps relied on direct fetches of pages already identified. Several pages could not be read (GOV.UK redirects, Optimal Workshop 101 pages, Brandeis and Radford case pages, NN/g first-click article, the Survey Research Methods paper host, Maze pricing) and are listed with status in appendix (d). Nothing below is invented; where a number is from a vendor summary rather than the original study, it says so.

## Key Question 1: Published discovery questionnaires, briefs and stakeholder interview guides

### Takeaway
Eleven published sources were read (two public-sector or research-grade, three practitioner guides, six agency or vendor templates). They converge on the same groups: why now, goals and success measures, audiences, what the current site does badly, competitors, content (who writes it, who owns it, what exists), brand and tone, technical constraints, languages, budget, timeline and who approves. The research-grade sources (GOV.UK, NN/g, Content Strategy Inc.) prefer short, open, identical opening questions asked in conversation, with a written survey only as a fallback because "response rates are typically lower". The agency templates are 11 to 119 questions long; the one explicit length recommendation is 20 to 30 questions.

### Cited Findings

**1. GOV.UK Service Manual, "How the discovery phase works" (primary, public body).** Audience: the service team and its stakeholders. Discovery is "the stage before committing to build"; "There's no set time period for a discovery, but around 4 to 8 weeks is typical." A discovery should learn about "your users and what they're trying to achieve", "any constraints you'd face making changes to how the service is run - for example because of technology or legislation", "the underlying policy intent you've been set up to address" and "opportunities to improve things - by sharing data with other teams, for example". To proceed you should "know how you'll measure whether you've been successful" and "have a list of ideas you'd like to test at alpha". It tells teams to involve "operations colleagues, because the user journey often includes offline channels" and says stopping after discovery is a valid outcome. No question list for stakeholders; it is user-research led. — [GOV.UK Service Manual](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-discovery-phase-works)

**2. Nielsen Norman Group, "Stakeholder Interviews 101" (Sarah Gibbons, 23 Oct 2022; research-grade practitioner guidance).** Audience: internal stakeholders. Format: "one-on-one, semistructured conversations" organised around four topics: success metrics, priorities, history and expertise, process and workflow. Verbatim questions: "Can you tell me a little bit about your role?"; "What does success look like for you and your team?"; "What challenges or business issues are currently top priorities for you? Your team?"; "Is there anything else that would be helpful for me to know?" Probes: "Tell me more about that…?", "Can you give me an example…?", "Why is that important to you?" Tone: frame it as a "coffee", ask for honesty, state that answers "won't be attributed to the person and that negative feedback is useful". Fallback: "If a live meeting isn't possible, email the top 4-5 questions. For large groups, a survey is an option, though response rates are typically lower." Analysis: informal summary for 5 or fewer stakeholders, thematic analysis for 5 or more. Always ask "Who else should I speak to?" — [NN/g Stakeholder Interviews 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/stakeholder-interviews/)

**3. Content Strategy Inc., "How to run stakeholder interviews" (Emma Bedard, 9 Jun 2021, updated Mar 2023; practitioner agency).** Audience: internal stakeholders. Length "20 to 45 minutes per interview, or one hour for each group discussion." Verbatim questions: "What is your current role, and how does it relate to this project?"; "How does this project impact you and your team?"; "In your own words, why is this project important?"; "Can you tell me what your definition of success is for this project?"; "What would you like to see six months from now to consider the project a success? And one year from now?" Rules: start every interview with the same questions so answers can be compared; "Only group together stakeholders if they have similar roles within the organization"; "Avoid pairing employees with their managers"; "Don't overcrowd group discussions" (groups of two to three). — [Content Strategy Inc.](https://contentstrategyinc.com/stakeholder-engagement-part-2-stakeholder-interviews/)

**4. A List Apart, "Audiences, Outcomes, and Determining User Needs" (Corey Vilhauer, 28 Feb 2012; practitioner).** Audience: client stakeholders at discovery. Verbatim: "Who do you feel are your site's audiences?"; "Who is currently visiting the site? What makes their visit a success in their eyes? In yours?"; "Who else is competing for their attention?"; "What do you want to persuade your audience to do?"; "What metrics do you want to keep track of?"; "What is your company's ultimate mission?"; "What message do you need to get across?"; "What has worked in the past? What hasn't worked in the past? What were the stumbling blocks?"; "What topics are off limits?"; "What is the current content workflow?"; "Who approves content?"; "What stumbling blocks are in place that make it difficult for the content to get published?"; "Who currently creates content?"; "Who will write it in the future?" — [A List Apart](https://alistapart.com/article/audiences-outcomes-and-determining-user-needs/)

**5. A List Apart, "Kick Ass Kickoff Meetings" (Kevin M. Hoffman, 3 Aug 2010; practitioner).** Audience: executives and project team at kickoff. Verbatim: "How does your organization define success? What is the role of the website/application in achieving that success?"; "Who is the one person that will make this project a success, and who is the greatest challenge?"; "What aspects of the internal culture or external environment could put this redesign/application at risk to fail?"; "Assuming we mitigate that risk, what would exceed your wildest dreams?" Asks interviewers to "reveal your interview subjects' specific, personal hopes and fears for the project" and to tell people "certain questions are 'off the record'". On executives: tailor sessions so a vice president does not "have to learn about complex server architecture goals in gory detail". — [A List Apart](https://alistapart.com/article/kick-ass-kickoff-meetings/)

**6. Brain Traffic, "Mission Possible: Stakeholder Alignment" (Kristina Halvorson, talk Dec 2017; practitioner).** Lists stakeholder interviews as one of four alignment tools (with project preparation, articulating strategy, navigating conflict); the framing questions are "Why are we creating this content?", "Who is it for?", "Who is accountable for its success?" No question list on the page (slides not in text). — [Brain Traffic](https://www.braintraffic.com/blog/mission-possible-stakeholder-alignment)

**7. HubSpot, "119 website redesign questions" (Madison Zoey Vettorino, updated 27 Sep 2023, first published 2015; vendor marketing).** Audience: agency to client. Groups: SMART goals, site requirements, functionality, benchmarks, lead generation, relationships, managing content, designer suggestions. Verbatim: "Why are you looking to redesign your site?"; "What is your business's unique value proposition?"; "What is the primary purpose of your website?"; "Who are your main three competitors?"; "Looking at their sites, which elements do you like and dislike?"; "What's the budget for this project?"; "What's the yearly budget for site improvements?"; "Do you need to support several languages?"; "What metric(s) do you use to measure the success of your entire website? Visits, conversions, or leads?"; "Do you have buyer personas? How many do you have and will we need to set up conversion funnels for each persona?"; "Will you update and reuse content or images from your current website?"; "Who is responsible for uploading and formatting the content for the site?"; "Who is managing the site after the relaunch?"; "Where is your site hosted?"; "Are we meeting Web Accessibility standards? (WCAG)". — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/agency/website-redesign-questions)

**8. Ybug, "32 questions to ask your client before you start" (Radim Hernych, 9 Jun 2026; vendor blog).** Groups: business and project goals (7), audience and competitors (6), design and brand (7), technical and content (8), logistics (4). Verbatim: "What is the primary goal of this website?"; "What action do you most want visitors to take when they arrive?"; "What does success look like six months after launch?"; "Why are you building or redesigning the website now?"; "What is not working about your current website?"; "Are there any pages, features, or sections that are non-negotiable?"; "Who is the primary audience for this website?"; "Are there secondary audiences? **On tone and jargon for executives (primary, GOV.UK content guidance):** "Research shows that people with higher levels of literacy prefer plain English."; "Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long."; "Avoid government 'buzzwords' and jargon."; "Where you need to use specialist terms, you can." but "Explain the term in plain English when you first use it." — [GOV.UK Use clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/). Tone: "Address the user as 'you' where possible."; "Write conversationally. Picture your audience and write as if you were talking to them one-to-one"; be "brisk, but not terse"; "adjectives can be subjective and make the text sound more emotive and like spin". — [GOV.UK Use the right tone](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/right-tone/)

### Inferences (mine)
- The strongest pattern for TIC is a two-layer approach: 30-45 minute conversations with the handful of directors who decide (NN/g, Content Strategy Inc.), and a short written questionnaire for the wider group of managers and offices, accepting that NN/g says survey response rates will be lower and planning reminders accordingly.
- Every template asks the same first question in different words ("why now", "what is not working", "top three frustrations"). Opening the TIC questionnaire with that question, identical for everyone, lets answers be compared across offices (Content Strategy Inc. rule).
- Executives read better when each question says why it is asked and gives an example answer (Mark Boulton practice in Smashing; GOV.UK "explain the term when you first use it"). The questionnaire should carry a one-line "why we ask" under each question.
- The agency templates are written for an agency asking a client. For TIC, where the person asking is an employee asking colleagues and directors, the NN/g "off the record / not attributed" framing and the Content Strategy Inc. rule "avoid pairing employees with their managers" matter more than any specific question.

### Gaps
- Webflow's own questionnaire article was not reached (search budget exhausted; vendor template page lists only topics). Brain Traffic's actual interview question list is in slides not available as text. The Typeform template's questions are only visible inside the product.
- GOV.UK's reading-age target (commonly quoted as age 9) is not on the current "Use clear language" page; only the 25-word sentence rule and the literacy statistic are. Do not quote a reading age from GOV.UK without finding the page that states it.
- No published questionnaire aimed specifically at inspection, testing or certification companies was found.

## Key Question 2: How multi-office organisations gather local input for one global site

### Takeaway
Published evidence is thin and mostly from universities and the TIC majors' own sites. The recurring lessons are: run scheduled sessions with every unit, back structural decisions with data, get the layer above the units (deans, regional directors) on board first, give units templates and a demo rather than a blank page, and accept that some units will resent losing control. Failures seen at TIC-adjacent companies are drift (country menus differing from the global one at Applus+ and Bureau Veritas, two IAs coexisting at TÜV SÜD) and decay when nobody owns a regional page.

### Cited Findings
- Xavier University redesign (HighEdWeb 2016 session by Rob Liesland, summarised by TerminalFour, 25 Oct 2016): central marketing "took back control of many sites that academic departments and support offices had maintained for years"; they "held scheduled sessions with all departments", "defined guidelines and a style guide, clarifying what could and could not be changed", and used "user polls" and analytics to justify changes. Lessons verbatim: "Communication was key as it was important to get their support"; "back it up with data"; "create style templates and also create a demo site"; "the team has to get the deans onboard above the offices and departments". What failed: "Some departments disliked losing control of their sites." — [TerminalFour blog](https://terminalfour.com/blog/posts/highedweb-2016---xavier-university-the-content-owner-debate.html)
- Case Western Reserve University web content management initiative (14 Sep 2011): a "brief survey for faculty and staff" that "takes 5-10 minutes", asking about "your department's web resources and needs", their experience as users and their view of current sites; about 90 university representatives had discussed the state of the web that summer; the initiative would "create workflows and a governance structure". — [Case Western](https://case.edu/news/web-content-management-initiative-move-facultystaff-survey-now-open)
- Search summaries (pages not opened): Radford University "identified and trained content owners and content editors for all Level 2 websites, covering colleges and departments" — [Radford](https://www.radford.edu/marketing-communication/our-services/digital-services/website-redesign-project.html) (fetch returned 404); Brandeis said "web editors will be part of this conversation through a brief survey" — [Brandeis](https://www.brandeis.edu/redesign/editors.html) (fetch returned 403); a practitioner article recommends "piloting with willing departments before rolling out broadly" — [William Alexander](https://williamalexander.co/articles/redesigning-department-sites-without-chaos/).
- Becton Dickinson global site (agency case, undated): deployed "globally across North America, Latin America, Europe, Africa and Asia" in "more than 10 international languages"; the search summary described "a series of collaborative workshops with stakeholders from each global regional office and business unit to identify needs and build an inventory of all content assets", but the page itself does not describe the workshops. — [Multimedia Solutions](https://www.multimediasolutions.com/work/becton-dickinson) (vendor)
- Deloitte Digital (Adobe case study, search summary; page fetch timed out): objective of "delivering consistent experiences while letting firms keep autonomy over their content", with content adoption across "142 regional websites". — [Adobe](https://business.adobe.com/customer-success-stories/deloitte-digital-case-study) (vendor)
- TIC-sector evidence from the existing notes: TÜV SÜD migrated "over 50 country sites into one, cohesive tuvsud.com" with "over 200 content authors" onboarded, yet its Middle East site still runs an older template with a different menu, so "two different IAs coexist on one domain" — [tuv-websites.md, citing tuvsud.com](https://www.tuvsud.com/en-ae); the Applus+ UAE site "has a different menu to the global one, which shows the drift risk" — [dnv-lr-applus-ul-websites.md]; Bureau Veritas "tolerates inconsistency across sites rather than enforcing one template" and its Middle East site is "English-only" with "a long static 'Our Offices' page" — [bureau-veritas-website.md]; "The recurring failure mode is governance, not design: regional sites and content hubs decay when nobody owns them" — [tuv-websites.md].
- Hub-and-spoke governance (vendor sources, from b2b-website-best-practice.md): central team owns brand, templates and legal pages; regional contributors own office pages, local news and translations; approvals tiered by risk — [Averi](https://resources.averi.ai/solutions/enterprise-content-operations), [Highspot](https://www.highspot.com/en-gb/blog/content-governance/).
- Office-profile fields seen on competitor sites (from existing notes): Element's location entries and UL's address/phone/map detail are the models; TIC "should prefer one global site with a location finder that filters by region/country and shows address, phone, email, services offered and accreditations per office" — [dnv-lr-applus-ul-websites.md]; Intertek keeps "office contact PDF per programme" as a workaround for a missing structured office directory — [sgs-intertek-websites.md].
- Content inventory fields (vendor, HubSpot, updated 3 Sep 2026): "Unique ID," "URL," "Page title and H1," "Meta description," "Content type," "Publication date and last updated date," "Content owner," "Status," "Primary keyword or topic," "Business goal," "Customer journey stage," "Primary call-to-action (CTA)," "Performance metrics," and "Audit action and notes"; audit actions "Keep, Update, Consolidate, Redirect, Remove, or Create"; status vocabulary "Published, Draft, Needs review, and Retired"; "Each URL should be assigned to a named person or team"; separate "page ownership from stewardship" (one inventory steward keeps the tracker's definitions and review cadence). — [HubSpot content inventory](https://blog.hubspot.com/website/content-inventory)
- On HQ-versus-local conflict resolution: the only published mechanism found is the Xavier one (style guide defining "what could and could not be changed", data, and sign-off from the layer above). Content Strategy Inc. adds a process rule for interviews: do not put employees and their managers in the same session. — sources above.
- On survey fatigue and response rates: no source specific to multi-office website consultations was found. General employee-survey vendor benchmarks (search summaries, not opened): "a good survey response rate usually falls between 70% and 80%" — [Sogolytics](https://www.sogolytics.com/learning-center/surveys/what-is-a-good-survey-response-rate/); "internal employee surveys often see response rates well above 50%, while external customer surveys frequently land between 10% and 30%" — [Pebb](https://pebb.io/insights/what-is-a-good-survey-response-rate); "the most useful benchmark is your own historical rate for a similar survey and audience" — [Simpplr](https://www.simpplr.com/blog/survey-benchmarks-response-rates/). NN/g: for large groups a survey works "though response rates are typically lower" than interviews. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/stakeholder-interviews/)

### Inferences (mine)
- For TIC's 13 offices the published pattern translates to: (1) directors first (the "deans" layer), (2) one identical office questionnaire plus a structured office-profile form, (3) a demo of the office page template so managers react to something concrete rather than describe from scratch, (4) a written rule on what an office may change (local contact, services offered locally, local accreditations, local news) and what it may not (menu, service names, brand, legal pages).
- The drift seen at Applus+, BV and TÜV SÜD is an argument for collecting office input as structured fields (the inventory in appendix b) that generate pages, rather than letting offices write free-form pages.
- Low response is the main risk, so the office form should be short (Case Western's "5-10 minutes" is a usable benchmark), bilingual, and chased by a named regional director rather than by the web lead.

### Gaps
- No case study was found from a law, engineering or inspection firm describing the actual form or fields used to collect office inputs; the office-profile field list in appendix (b) is assembled from competitor office pages and inventory practice, not from a published template.
- No published evidence on response rates for executive-only or manager-only surveys; only general employee-survey vendor benchmarks.
- The Deloitte and BD case pages do not describe how local autonomy was bounded; the "what may change locally" rule above is inferred.

## Key Question 3: Validating menus and service names with executives and customers

### Takeaway
Card sorting (generative) and tree testing (evaluative) are the standard methods; NN/g says 15 participants give a 0.90 correlation for card sorts and recommends 30-50 cards; Optimal Workshop (vendor) recommends 30-50 card-sort participants and about 50 completed tree tests, with 20-30 enough for directional findings. All run remotely and unmoderated, so busy executives in 13 countries can take part by email link in 10-15 minutes. Tool licences start at about US$166-199 per month (Lyssna Growth, Optimal Workshop Starter), with free tiers that fit a small internal study. Highlighter tests and five-second tests need no tool at all and have been used with internal experts (18F ran one with two attorneys).

### Cited Findings
- Card sorting definition (NN/g, Tankala and Sherwin, 2 Feb 2024): open sorts let participants create their own groups and labels ("We recommend this approach because you get a holistic view of users' mental models"); closed sorts use predefined categories and are "for validation, though the article generally recommends tree testing instead"; hybrid is generally advised against; moderated for qualitative insight, unmoderated for quantitative data; remote is "more common because it reaches geographically diverse participants"; "30-50 cards" to prevent fatigue, with over 50 cards participants overuse a "Miscellaneous" category; participants "at least 15" for qualitative, "at least 30-50" for quantitative; tools: OptimalSort, Zoom, Microsoft Teams. — [NN/g card sorting](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-definition/)
- Card sort sample size (NN/g, Jakob Nielsen, 18 Jul 2004): correlation with the final structure 0.75 at 5 users, 0.90 at 15, 0.95 at 30, 0.98 at 60; "You must test 15 users to reach a correlation of 0.90, which is a more comfortable place to stop"; after 15 "diminishing returns set in"; "around 30 users" for large well-funded projects; good results "with as few as 12 users when budgets were tight"; based on one study (Tullis and Wood, 168 users) and "It's only one study." — [NN/g how many users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-how-many-users-to-test/)
- Tree testing (NN/g, Page Laubheimer, 6 Aug 2023, reviewed 19 Aug 2026): "task-based research method for checking whether users can find resources in a proposed navigation hierarchy" using a text-only tree, no design needed; use it "at the start of a redesign, to benchmark the current information architecture", "after a card sort" and "before writing content or designing layouts"; qualitative tests need "only a few participants", quantitative "a larger sample size" (no number given); quantitative tests "are often run remotely and unmoderated" but do "a few moderated pilot sessions first, plus a short post-test survey asking which labels were confusing"; metrics: success rate, time on task, directness, wrong categories chosen; task wording must not contain the exact label ("Gives away the answer"); tools UserZoom and Treejack (Optimal Workshop). — [NN/g tree testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tree-testing/)
- Tree-test sample size (Optimal Workshop support article, vendor): "50 - 100 participants per task will give you trustworthy data"; "the trends in participant responses should start to come clear with around 50 participants"; fewer than 50 still yields "plenty of actionable insights"; card sorting "we recommend aiming for 30 - 50 participants"; "15-30 participants are adequate for qualitative research"; worked example: 500 users, 10% margin of error, 95% confidence needs 81 participants. — [Optimal Workshop support](https://support.optimalworkshop.com/en/articles/9679633-how-many-participants-you-need-for-reliable-results). Tree Testing 101 recruitment page (vendor): "Ideally you'll have around 50 participants complete your tree tests", "you can still get plenty of useful insights with fewer participants"; recruit "employees if you're working on an internal product, or your customer mailing list"; "If you say an activity will take five minutes at the most, people will take that quite literally." — [Optimal Workshop Tree Testing 101](https://www.optimalworkshop.com/101-guides/tree-testing-101/recruit-participants). Search summary of the product FAQ: "as few as 20-30 participants can surface the most significant problems" for exploratory studies; "aim for 50 per group" when comparing trees. — [Optimal Workshop product page](https://www.optimalworkshop.com/product/tree-testing)
- Other tree-test minimums (search summaries): University of Glasgow UX framework suggests 15-50 remote users with 10 as the minimum — [Glasgow](https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/ux/deliver/treetesting); European Commission Europa Web Guide advises up to 50 for unmoderated — [EC Europa Web Guide](https://commission.europa.eu/resources/europa-web-guide/planning-your-web-presence/architecture-and-navigation/architecture-building-and-testing-methods/tree-testing-method_en); a tree-testing wiki suggests 8-10 tasks per participant — [treetesting.atlassian.net](https://treetesting.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/TTFW/pages/164260); a US federal plan targeted 30-50 completions and auto-closed at 50 — [omb.report](https://omb.report/icr/202608-0906-005/doc/171752800.pdf).
- Five-second test (Lyssna guide, vendor): "participants view a design for five seconds" (up to 20 in the tool), then answer "What was this page about?", "What product or service was being offered?", "Who do you think this is designed for?", "What elements stood out most?", "Was anything unclear or confusing?"; "Limit the total number of questions to prevent participant fatigue (5-8 questions maximum)"; "Five to ten participants typically provide enough data to identify major patterns and issues"; limits: "doesn't explain the underlying reasons", "can't measure task completion rates". — [Lyssna five-second testing](https://lyssna.com/guides/five-second-testing). Origins (search summaries): Jared Spool, Christine Perfetti and Tom Tullis; later popularised by Paul Doncaster via UsabilityHub — [UXpod interview](https://uxpod.com/episodes/designing-good-five-second-tests-an-interview-with-paul-doncaster.html); keep to about three questions, no more than five — [DesignHammer](https://designhammer.com/resources/usability-guide/five-second-test).
- Content highlighter test (18F Methods, US government, primary): "Users highlight text that they think is clear in one color" then confusing text in another; purpose "To understand which words and phrases are achieving their intended purpose"; "You can conduct this test in person, on paper, or online"; steps: select content, select a tool, define what you're testing (clarity, or confidence versus uncertainty), pick colours, write instructions stating "how long the test will take", run, discuss ("Encourage users to describe why they highlighted passages"), analyse. Worked example on pra.digital.gov: "Two attorneys reviewed it for clarity and accuracy using the highlighter method" and "this test allowed us to avoid a later legal review." — [18F content highlighter testing](https://guides.18f.org/methods/validate/content-highlighter-testing/). Welsh government variant uses colours for emotions (confident vs confusing) — [Digital Public Services Wales](https://digitalpublicservices.gov.wales/node/561); 1-2 hours per test per the search summary of the same 18F page.
- Tool prices (vendor pages, 2026-10-21): Optimal Workshop Starter "$199/mo" "Billed annually", "5 studies launched per year", "Unlimited seats", "Unlimited participant responses per study", "All tools and study types available on all plans" (card sorting, tree testing, first-click); Enterprise custom; recruitment 1 credit per participant. — [Optimal Workshop pricing](https://www.optimalworkshop.com/pricing). ## Key Question 4: Questionnaire design evidence for non-technical respondents

### Takeaway
Pew's rules (one question at a time, exhaustive and mutually exclusive options, short lists of four or five, avoid agree/disagree, pretest) and GOV.UK's plain-language rules (sentences under 25 words, explain specialist terms on first use, "people with higher levels of literacy prefer plain English") are the primary guidance. Length evidence is mixed: announced length reduces starts (75% start a 10-minute survey vs 62% for 30 minutes in Galesic and Bosnjak 2009, via secondary summaries), later questions get "faster, shorter, and more uniform" answers, but Dynata reports response rate "does not depend on interview length" (own unpublished data). Ranking questions should hold 6-10 items and only the top and bottom three positions are reliable (Qualtrics). For Arabic, the team-translation method (TRAPD) is recommended over back-translation, written in Modern Standard Arabic with pretesting by native speakers of the local dialect.

### Cited Findings
- Pew Research Center, "Writing survey questions" (primary methodology page): weak questions put data on "a shaky foundation of ambiguous or biased questions"; open-ended lets people answer in their own words, closed-ended offers a list; format changes answers (58% chose "the economy" when offered vs 35% volunteered it, 2008); Pew "sometimes runs open-ended pilots to find common answers, then builds closed-ended options from them"; keep option lists to "just four or perhaps five at most"; Pew "generally avoids select-all-that-apply questions" (a 2019 study found forced-choice more accurate); ask one thing at a time (its double-barrelled example asks about "domestic and foreign policy" together); options "exhaustive and mutually exclusive"; "clear, concrete language", no double negatives or jargon; agree/disagree formats "invite acquiescence bias"; order effects exist, so group by topic, start with engaging items and put demographics late; "Pretesting a survey is an essential step in the questionnaire design process." Ranking questions are not discussed. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/our-methods/u-s-surveys/writing-survey-questions/)
- GOV.UK "Use clear language" (primary): "Research shows that people with higher levels of literacy prefer plain English."; "Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long."; "Avoid government 'buzzwords' and jargon."; "Where you need to use specialist terms, you can." and "Explain the term in plain English when you first use it." — [GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/). "Use the right tone": "Address the user as 'you' where possible."; "Write conversationally. Picture your audience and write as if you were talking to them one-to-one"; "brisk, but not terse". — [GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/right-tone/)
- Questionnaire length (Galesic and Bosnjak 2009, Public Opinion Quarterly 73(2), 349-360, as summarised by secondary sources; original not opened): announced lengths of 10, 20 and 30 minutes in an opt-in web survey in Croatia; "75% started the 10-minute survey, compared with 65% for the 20-minute survey and 62% for the 30-minute survey"; "answers to questions positioned later in the questionnaire were faster, shorter, and more uniform than answers to questions positioned near the beginning." — [Angus Reid white paper](https://angusreid.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/The-Hidden-Costs-of-Long-Surveys-Why-Survey-Length-Impacts-Data-Quality.pdf), [SMG paper](https://smg.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/increasing_response_rates_by_managing_survey_length.pdf), [Sandelin 2022, Gothenburg](https://www.gu.se/sites/default/files/2022-11/2022-1%20Effects%20of%20questionnaire%20length%20%28Sandelin%202022%29%20v2_1.pdf) (PDF downloaded but could not be parsed locally). Yan et al. (2011) "found higher break-off rates for a 25-minute survey compared with a 16-minute one" (same secondary sources).
- Dropout figures from market-research vendors (search summaries, not opened; sample sizes not stated): dropout 12% for a 10-minute survey vs 40% for 30 minutes; straight-lining 5% vs 20%; another summary gives 31.8% (10 min), 43.2% (20 min), 53.2% (30 min) — [Drive Research](https://driveresearch.com/market-research-company-blog/what-impacts-survey-response-rates-a-closer-look), [Into the Minds](https://www.intotheminds.com/blog/en/online-survey-participation-factors/), [DJS Research](https://djsresearch.co.uk/news/how-to-determine-the-right-survey-length). Suggested maximums range from 10 to 15-20 minutes — [Mili](https://www.mili.eu/learn/optimal-survey-length-how-long-survey-lengths-can-affect-data-quality), [QuestionPro](https://www.questionpro.com/blog/survey-length-keep-it-short-and-simple/).
- Contradicting view: Dynata (vendor blog, 26 Jan 2022, own data, no figures): "Response rate does not depend on interview length"; most dropout "has occurred by the halfway stage, irrespective of interview length"; mobile respondents' dropout does rise with length. — [Dynata](https://www.dynata.com/survey-length-best-practices-are-shorter-surveys-better/)
- Ranking questions (Qualtrics, vendor guidance): "Try to limit your ranking question options to between 6 and 10 items"; "you can expect reasonably reliable rankings for the top three and bottom three items"; "the middle rankings tend to be much more noisy and unreliable"; "If you're looking for a value against each item, you're better off using a rating question"; respondents "can be forced to rank all options, some options, or rule-based options"; offering "n/a" makes results more accurate; "consider using broad categories to group the items" for long lists; pre-test formats on different devices. — [Qualtrics ranking dos and don'ts](https://www.qualtrics.com/blog/the-dos-and-donts-of-ranking-questions/). Other vendors put the comfortable range at 5-8 or 3-8 items — [Sogolytics](https://www.sogolytics.com/learning-center/survey-questionnaire/ranking-questions-in-surveys/), [SurveyMethods](https://surveymethods.com/kb/ranking/).
- Internal survey length precedent: Case Western's faculty/staff web survey "takes 5-10 minutes". — [Case Western](https://case.edu/news/web-content-management-initiative-move-facultystaff-survey-now-open)
- Translation method, TRAPD (Walde and Völlm, Frontiers in Psychiatry 14:1199989, 15 Jun 2023, peer-reviewed): "TRAPD is an acronym for the steps of the translation process, specifically Translation, Review, Adjudication, Pretest, and Documentation."; back-translation's "ability to detect flaws in translation and necessities for cultural adaptation is limited"; translators "who lack context or field-specific terminology may both choose the same wrong term, so the error goes undetected"; team: several translators with different backgrounds translating independently, then a joint review with survey-methodology experts, then an adjudicator "who knows the project but did not take part in the translation"; pretest with 17 respondents in two rounds (9 then 8). — [PMC10309006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10309006/)
- Pew's own translation practice (primary, 22 Apr 2022): translation assessment (flag idioms, simplify, e.g. "closer to" became "most similar to"), translation by the local field agency, line-by-line verification by a separate firm with "at least two rounds" of discussion, pretesting with respondents, documentation; rejects back-translation alone because "The back translation might smooth out the poor translation choices made by the initial translator"; "Pew Research Center does not have a team of trained linguists on staff". Arabic is not mentioned. — [Pew Decoded](https://www.pewresearch.org/decoded/2022/04/22/how-we-translate-survey-questions-to-be-fielded-around-the-world/)
- Arabic-specific practice (search summaries of peer-reviewed adaptation studies, not opened): an Algerian study chose Modern Standard Arabic because MSA "can be spoken, but is primarily used only as a written language" and ran cognitive interviews with a native speaker of the local dialect — [Dawsey et al. 2014 poster](https://healthpsychologyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Poster_Dawsey-et-al.-2014_ISPOR_LV_Algeria.pdf); the ASQ-3 Arabic version was reviewed by experts from "Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Egypt" so it works across dialects — [ASQ-3 Arabic note](https://llm.agesandstages.com/content/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/about-the-asq-3-arabic-questionnaires-pdf.md); a Saudi study used cognitive interviewing including probes for perceived sensitivity — [PubMed 37307255](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37307255/). No Gulf-business guideline on dialect choice was found.
- Open vs closed in practice (NN/g): email "the top 4-5 questions" when a meeting is impossible; surveys for large groups have lower response. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/stakeholder-interviews/)

### Inferences (mine)
- A manager questionnaire of 10-15 minutes, roughly 20-30 questions (Ybug's range, consistent with Case Western's 5-10 minutes for a shorter form), with the announced length printed on the invitation, is the evidence-backed shape. Longer forms lose starts and degrade later answers.
- Use "top 3" ranking only on short lists (6-10 items, Qualtrics) such as "which three services bring your office the most enquiries", and read only the top positions. For longer lists (TIC's 40-50 service names) use a card sort (Key Question 3), not a ranking question.
- Avoid agree/disagree statements about the current site (Pew's acquiescence warning); ask forced choices or open "what is not working" questions instead.
- Pew's open-pilot-then-closed sequence fits TIC's two rounds: director interviews (open) generate the option lists for the wider closed questionnaire.
- For Arabic: write the questionnaire in English, translate into MSA, have two bilingual staff in different offices (e.g. Cairo and Baghdad or Amman) review independently, let a third adjudicate, pretest with two or three Arabic-speaking managers, and keep a log of wording decisions (TRAPD). Back-translation alone is not enough.

### Gaps
- The original Galesic and Bosnjak paper and its completion/break-off figures were not read; only start rates and the quality finding are cited, from secondary summaries.
- No published response-rate benchmark for executive or manager surveys; only employee-engagement vendor ranges.
- No Arabic-specific business-survey guideline on MSA versus dialect for Gulf executives; the practice above is transferred from health research.
- Harvard and Qualtrics general survey guides were not opened (search budget).

## Key Question 5: Collecting comments and approvals on a 20-40 page proposal from 15-30 people in several countries

### Takeaway
Each mechanism trades ease for control. Word attachments by email are familiar but create version sprawl and heavy consolidation; a single co-authored Word file on OneDrive/SharePoint (or a Google Doc) keeps one version with tracked changes, named comments and version history, but requires everyone to be online, invited and comfortable in the document; a form per section (Microsoft Forms, Google Forms, Typeform) gives structured, countable answers and optional anonymity but loses the context of the page being commented on; review tools (Filestage, Ziflow) add approvals, deadlines and version comparison at about US$199-329 per month and are built for marketing assets; a wiki/Notion page suits an evolving document with page history (7 days free, 30-90 days paid). Decisions should be logged in a DACI/decision-log or ADR format, never deleted, with superseded entries marked.

- For TIC the practical combination is: (1) one master proposal document in the company's existing Microsoft 365 or Google tenant (whichever the offices already use; this is an internal unknown), shared read-only with comment rights, so there is one version and every comment is named and dated; (2) a short section-by-section form (Microsoft or Google Forms) for managers who will not comment inside a document, with "Record name" on so answers can be followed up (anonymity is better handled in the interviews than in the form); (3) a PDF copy by email only for anyone who cannot access the shared file, with a rule that emailed comments are transcribed into the master by the web lead the same week; (4) a decision log in DACI/ADR form kept beside the proposal, one entry per decision (menu structure, service names, languages, domain, platform), each with the options considered, who approved and the date, never deleted.
- Filestage/Ziflow are built for marketing assets and video; for a one-off 20-40 page proposal their monthly cost (US$199-329) buys approval stamps and deadline reminders that SharePoint/Google plus a form already give. They become worth considering later for recurring website page approvals across 13 offices, not for this proposal round.
- Consolidation effort is the hidden cost of Word-by-email: with 15-30 reviewers it means merging 15-30 files by hand. Co-authoring or comments on one shared file removes that step; the Track Changes "lock" option prevents a reviewer from switching tracking off.
- Executives in several countries will read on phones; comments in Word mobile or the Google Docs app are possible but the form route is more likely to be completed on a phone. Offer both.

- From regional-and-midsize-competitors.md: whether COSQC's official product list can be linked and whether COSQC fees may be stated; what competitors show inside client portals is unknown, so TIC's own portal requirements must come from operations. — [regional-and-midsize-competitors.md]
- Halal: the Halal page claims an "i-CAS Halal Scheme" and "ISO/IEC 17065 accredited" status without naming the accreditation body; the user-journeys persona asks which body backs it and in which destinations (UAE, KSA, Malaysia, Indonesia) it is recognised. — [ticqc-current-site-audit.md], [users-and-journeys.md]

### Inferences (mine)
- About a third of these unknowns are documents (certificates, scopes, decrees, forms, policies) rather than opinions; they should be collected through the office/service inventory (appendix b) with a "please attach" field, not through the questionnaire.
- The three unknowns that block the proposal's tech side (in-house lead's hours and skills; hosting-residency clauses in programme contracts; where certificate records live) sit with three specific people and should be asked in person before the wider round, because every platform recommendation depends on them.

### Gaps
- The notes do not record who at TIC currently holds each programme (Iraq, Egypt, Botswana, Libya, SFDA, Halal, CSUAS); the audience column in appendix (a) names roles, not people.
