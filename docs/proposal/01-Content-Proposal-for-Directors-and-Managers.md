Our website, ticqc.ae, was built in June 2024, when we were a smaller company. ## 1. Why now

### 1.1 The honest state of ticqc.ae

What our research found, stated plainly. None of this is anyone's fault. The site was built quickly, for a smaller company, and maintained by people with other jobs to do.

- About 49 pages exist, but only about 20 can be reached from the menu. The Testing page is missing from the main menu, and its six sub-pages, the NDT page, the Saudi SFDA page, the Agri and Sustainability pages, eight product inspection pages and ten training course pages are reachable only through a search engine or a link buried in another page.
- The "Government Services Programs" card on the home page, and the same link in every footer, lead to a page-not-found error.
- There is no privacy policy, no terms, no careers page, no accreditation page, no office pages, no certificate check, no client login and no version in any language other than English (a Portuguese version exists, but only as an empty English shell).
- The home page weighs about 9 megabytes (the amount a visitor must download, as measured by Google's PageSpeed tool). On a mid-range phone the main picture takes 20 to 30 seconds to appear. Almost all of the weight is images, including one 3.6 MB photo and logos uploaded at ten times their displayed size.
- The footer shows nine accreditation and membership logos with no certificate numbers, scopes or links to registers. Section 5.6 explains why this matters.
- The About page has no founding year, no leadership names and no group structure, and mentions "Seacorr" twice where "TIC Quality Control" was meant.
- The 2024 industry pages are short (350 to 650 words) with no form and no questions-and-answers. The 2026 program pages are long and detailed, with good FAQs and downloadable forms, but no online application. The two eras look like two websites.
- The footer lists 14 addresses in 12 countries, with phone numbers for five of them and no readable email.
- One page is published still carrying the page builder's placeholder title instead of a real one. Typos sit in the footer and in several addresses.
- The software underneath the site is out of date and no longer receives security fixes. The technology document explains; here it is one more reason not to wait.

### 1.2 What we already have going for us

The company is stronger than the site. We hold an ANAB accreditation to ISO/IEC 17020 (the international standard for inspection bodies) with a public certificate number, valid to October 2028, covering Dubai, New Delhi, Amman, Guangzhou and Riyadh. We are in the Egyptian GOEIC register of approved inspection companies. We hold a COSQC appointment for Iraq under a 2024 contract (COSQC publishes no public list, so the letter itself is our proof). Our Iraq page carries more questions-and-answers than any of the big international companies publish. None of this appears on the site in a form a visitor can check.

### 1.3 The India office's blueprint

In September 2026 our India office prepared a "Technical Website Specification and SEO Blueprint". It is a thorough, well-organised chapter on how search engines should find our pages: clean web addresses, one topic per page, breadcrumb trails, sitemaps, fast images, a certificate check, a single contact page with all offices, and a blog with named authors. I wrote a plain-language guide to it, which several of you have read.

This proposal builds on that chapter and adds what a full website plan also needs: all our business lines in one menu, what every page says, who writes and owns it, languages, how we handle enquiries, and the order of building. Where this document departs from the blueprint (the fixed 1,000-word rule, share buttons on every page) section 12 says so and why.

## 2. What we learned from the world's TIC companies

We looked at SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, TÜV SÜD, TÜV Rheinland, TÜV NORD, DNV, Lloyd's Register, Applus+, UL, Kiwa and Element, and then at the companies that sell the same government programs we sell: Cotecna, HQTS, Baltic Control, Sunchine and QIMA. Seventeen sites in all; the full list and every detail are in the research report.

### 2.1 What they all have

| Feature | Who has it | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| A short menu: Services first, Industries second | Every current site we studied | Visitors look for what they need first, who they are second. |
| A public "verify a certificate" tool | 10 of the 17 sites, including SGS, Kiwa, Cotecna and HQTS | A customs officer or bank checks a certificate without calling us. Our only verification link returns page-not-found. |
| A client login | 10 of the 17 sites, including SGS, Intertek and Cotecna | Clients fetch their own reports. For us a later phase (5.10). |

| Public impartiality and complaints-and-appeals pages | SGS (on every program page), Bureau Veritas, TÜV Rheinland, Applus+, TÜV NORD | Regulators and assessors look for these. We have a PDF in the footer and no complaints page. |
| One calm design: one photo, one accent colour, one or two typefaces used consistently, real photos of their own people | Kiwa, Element, Cotecna (SGS as the large-corporate reference) | Professional on a phone and fast. ### 2.2 What nobody does well

| Gap | Evidence | Our opening |
|---|---|---|
| The languages of our markets | The majors do not publish in the languages of the markets they serve: Arabic for Egypt and Saudi Arabia, Indian languages such as Hindi for exporters in India, Chinese for exporters in Guangzhou. SGS, Intertek, Cotecna and Bureau Veritas have no Arabic pages for Egypt or Saudi Arabia, and their Indian and Chinese pages are English or thin; TÜV Rheinland's UAE Arabic site is partial and shows "Translation missing". Where a competitor does translate, it is usually a machine overlay with no typeface chosen for the script. | A properly built version in any of these languages, reviewed by our own colleagues in that office, would give visitors in that market something none of the large companies offers them today. Which languages, and in which order, is for the offices to request (5.12). |
| Published fees and turnaround | No competitor states a fee or a typical number of days on the program page itself. Bureau Veritas and TÜV Rheinland put a fee table inside a downloadable datasheet, and HQTS gives a document-review time of one to two working days. Our Libya page is the only page in the set that prints the fee on the page. Research on business websites names hidden pricing as the most frustrating omission for buyers. | Where the regulator permits, publishing fees and days on the page, in plain sight, would make our program pages more useful than any competitor's. A leadership decision (D5). |
| A human on WhatsApp | Only QIMA offers a WhatsApp link. Nobody publishes hours or promises a human. About 90 percent of people in the UAE use WhatsApp, and in one UAE survey nine in ten preferred a human to a chatbot. | Low cost, and no competitor offers it yet. Offices: tell me if your clients use a different channel (Telegram or Viber in Iraq, for example). |

### 2.3 Who sets the achievable bar

The giants run on enterprise software with hundreds of pages per country and large teams. We learn from their program pages, not their size. The three sites that show what a company of our size can achieve are **Kiwa**, **Element** and **Cotecna**:

- **Kiwa**: the clearest accreditations page (a list of registration numbers, each linked to the accreditation body's register; our table will go one better with certificate downloads and a last-checked date), a certificate finder, a "receive a quote" bar on every service page, and the only one of the seventeen sites we studied that Google's own measurements show loading quickly for ordinary visitors.
- **Element**: the best offices page (about 159 locations, list and map) and named experts on service pages.
- **Cotecna**: closest to our business. Government programs, a client portal, certificate authentication, three languages, and a "send us a request" card on every program page with the request form, product list and complaints guidelines as downloads.

None of them did anything exotic. One or two typefaces, one accent colour, one photo, applied everywhere. Our brand book already gives us the same ingredients: Saira for headings, Open Sans for text, one red.

## 3. Who visits our website and what they need

Some visitors bring revenue (exporters, forwarders, traders). Some bring trust or risk (customs officers, regulators, procurement teams). Each needs a different first page and a different way to reach us.

| Visitor | What they want, in one line | How they want to contact us | Language and device |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exporter or manufacturer needing a Certificate of Conformity or Inspection (CoC / CoI) for Iraq, Egypt, Botswana or Libya | Is my product regulated, what documents, how many days, what cost, nearest office to my factory | In their own language and on WhatsApp or WeChat, then phone; wants to submit today | English, Arabic, Indian languages such as Hindi, or Chinese; mostly on a phone in India, Pakistan, South-East Asia; desktop in China |
| Importer or customs officer checking a certificate | Is this certificate genuine and issued by an appointed body | A lookup page; a number to call if it does not match | Their own language (Arabic in Iraq and Egypt, English in Botswana); phone at the border |
| Freight forwarder or customs broker | Which body is approved for this destination, can you inspect at my port on this date, one link to send my client | Fast reply from a named coordinator | English plus local language; phone and desktop |
| Commodity trader (grain, rice, sugar, oils) | Are you a GAFTA approved superintendent in my country, which ports, weight, sampling, draft survey | Email or phone to the port office; a named surveyor | English; desktop |
| Food importer or manufacturer wanting halal | Is your certificate recognised in my destination, who accredits you, process and cost | Apply online; verify a certificate | English, Arabic, Malay; phone |
| Procurement officer prequalifying us | ISO certificates, accreditation scope, trade licence, insurance, HSE policy, company profile, all dated, in one place | Download a pack | English, sometimes Arabic; desktop |
| Existing client wanting a report | Where is my certificate, what is my status, who is my contact | WhatsApp or email; later a login | Own language; phone |
| Regulator or accreditation assessor | Is the public information consistent with your scope; are impartiality and complaints policies published | Reads and records | English, Arabic; desktop |
| Job seeker or freelance inspector | Are you hiring in my city, what do you need, how to apply | Apply online, upload a CV | Local language; phone |
| Partner inspection body or laboratory | Do you subcontract in my country, what are your requirements | Email the right person | English; desktop |
| Journalist or analyst | What is TIC, how big, where, which accreditations, who leads it | A press contact and a fact sheet | English; desktop |

Two things follow. The exporter arrives under time pressure, often with a shipment already booked, so the best pages are checklists, not brochures. And the people who check us need proof they can verify in a minute, which is why "Verify a certificate" and the accreditation table sit at the same level as the service pages.

**Working assumption (Darya):** the five visitor types that drive most revenue are the exporter, the importer or customs officer, the forwarder, the commodity trader and the existing client. Tell me if your office sees a different mix.

## 4. What the new website promises

Six promises. Everything else follows from them.

1. **Find any service in two clicks.** Any service, program or office is at most two clicks from the home page. No page is hidden from the menu.
2. **Prove everything we claim with a number and a link.** Every accreditation, approval and membership logo sits beside its certificate number, scope, expiry date and a link to the public register. If we cannot link it, we do not show it.
3. **Speak the visitor's language, when an office asks for it.** English is the one language every page has. Any added language (Arabic, Indian, Chinese, Portuguese or another) is a full, reviewed version of the pages that office's visitors use, never a machine overlay, and it is added when an office requests it and names a colleague to review it, exactly as the Brazil office did with Portuguese.
4. **Answer on WhatsApp and by phone, with a human, within published hours.** Every office page shows its channels and hours. We promise a reply time and keep it.
5. **Every page has a named owner and a review date.** Program pages show "last reviewed" and the office responsible.
6. **It works on a cheap phone on a slow connection.** Home page in under three seconds on a mid-range phone; every page readable without zooming.

Promises 3 and 4 commit offices and leadership to real hours. They are working assumptions until the decisions in section 13 are answered.

**Why Services first and Industries second.** Every current website we studied, from SGS to Kiwa, leads with Services. A visitor knows what they need ("a pre-shipment inspection", "an Iraq CoC") more precisely than which industry label we would give them. Industries is a second door for the visitor who thinks "I am a food importer, what do you do for people like me?". Both menus point at the same pages.

**Why Government & Trade Programs stands alone.** It is our largest and most searched-for line, and Intertek, Bureau Veritas, TÜV Rheinland and Cotecna all give it its own hub. Today it sits inside Services, which is one reason the broken home-page link stayed broken for months: it was easy to miss. A top-level item is seen every day.

**An alternative menu.** We could keep "Business Assurance" as a top item, the label we use today for halal and CSUAS:

Services | Industries | Government & Trade Programs | Business Assurance | About | Contact

It keeps a familiar word. The risk is that at SGS and Intertek "Business Assurance" means ISO certification and audits, so a visitor expecting that will find halal and drones and be confused.

**Decision needed (D1):** which menu reads better for our customers?

**Working assumption (Darya):** the six-item menu, with halal, audits and certification under Services.

### 5.5 Government & Trade Programs

These are our Certificate of Conformity and Certificate of Inspection programs, the ones our current site groups under "Government Services Programs" (GSP). This is where we are measured against Intertek, Bureau Veritas, TÜV Rheinland, Cotecna and HQTS, and where we can lead. Each program gets one page built from the same twelve-part recipe. Every part is something at least one competitor does.

1. **The regulator and our appointment**, with the date and the document (the COSQC letter as a PDF, the GOEIC register entry as a link).
2. **Who must comply**, and the regulated product list with the date of the version shown.
3. **Routes** (shipment-by-shipment, registration for regular shippers, licensing) with a simple helper: three questions that tell the exporter which route applies.
4. **Step-by-step process**, with typical working days per step.
5. **Document checklist**, with the request form completed online rather than downloaded as a Word file.
6. **Testing, inspection and labelling requirements** (for example Arabic labelling and origin marks for Iraq).
7. **Fees**, printed on the page, or a fee-structure download, where the regulator permits. Our Libya page already prints the fee.
8. **Questions and answers.** Our Iraq page has 23; we keep and improve them.
9. **Regulatory updates**, a dated list of changes.
10. **Contacts per office** handling this program, with WhatsApp, phone and a named coordinator.
11. **Complaints and appeals** link.
12. **Last reviewed date**, owning office, short disclaimer.

Programs we run today:

## 8. Photography, tone and look, in non-design words

Detailed design comes only after this proposal is approved. The website follows the TIC Brand Guidelines 2026 (Edition 1, October 2026), owned by Marketing & Design at HQ Dubai; the design brief will apply them, not reinvent them. The brand book already answers most design questions, so the design phase will be about layout and content, not about colours and fonts. In plain words, these are the web rules it sets:

- **One red, used sparingly.** TIC Red is an accent for rules, bullets, one highlighted figure and the call-to-action button. Charcoal, black, steel and white do the work. A page that is more than about a tenth red is shouting.
- **Two typefaces, plus one per added script.** Headings in Saira capitals; body text in Open Sans. When an office requests a language in another script, that script gets one matching typeface (Cairo for Arabic when requested, per the brand book; a typeface chosen with the office for Indian languages such as Hindi, for Chinese, or for any other script). Nothing else.
- **Square corners, no shadows, no gradients.** Boxes, photos and buttons are rectangular. The only gradient allowed is the approved red of the tagline band.
- **The header and footer are fixed.** The header carries the horizontal lockup with the words "TIC QUALITY CONTROL" beside the logo. The footer is a dark band ending with the TESTING | INSPECTION | CERTIFICATION tagline band on every page.
- **Lists and steps.** Red diamond bullets for lists and red triangle markers for process steps and "next" links; no round dots.
- **The logo is never redrawn.** Only the supplied logo files are used, in the scheme the background calls for.
- **Real photographs of our inspectors at work.** Ports, container yards, factories and labs, in daylight or clean industrial light. No handshakes, no staged smiles, no generic skylines, and no computer-generated images presented as real. Only licensed stock or our own photos with the permission of the people shown.

Three content rules of my own sit alongside them, and they are working assumptions (Darya) that you may overturn:

- **No sliding banners, no animated counters.** One photo, one sentence.
- **Plain English.** Short sentences, technical terms explained in brackets, as I have tried to do here. Headings are short so that they still read well in Saira capitals; body text and labels are in sentence case.
- **Readable on a cheap phone.** Text large enough, buttons a thumb can hit, good contrast. The brand book's allowed colour pairs already pass the contrast test; on our Iraq page today a testing tool found 204 places where the text is too faint against its background to read easily, all caused by one colour choice the new system will not offer.

Each office will be asked for photos in the inventory form. Where we have none, we arrange a short photo session rather than buy stock.
